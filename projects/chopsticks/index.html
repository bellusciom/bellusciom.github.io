Chopsticks Is Unwinnable

A case analysis showing that a player can always escape the winning pattern in Chopsticks.

Chopsticks is a two-player hand game. Each player holds out two hands, and on each turn a player either attacks — adding the finger count of one of their live hands to one of the opponent's live hands — or splits, redistributing their own total finger count between their two hands. A hand that accumulates five or more fingers is dead (set to $0$), and a player with two dead hands loses. The analysis below shows that a win can never be forced: the defending player can always escape the pattern from which a winning attack is delivered.

Notation

Write $h_i = xyzw$ for the state of the hands before the $i$-th move, where

  • $x$: number on your left hand,
  • $y$: number on your right hand,
  • $z$: number on opponent's left hand,
  • $w$: number on opponent's right hand.

Moves are written $T_i : n \to m$ or $n \to n,m$, denoting an attack ($n \to m$: hand $n$ strikes hand $m$) or a split ($n \to n,m$: the total on the mover's hands is redistributed between hands $n$ and $m$), with hands numbered $1,2,3,4$ for $x,y,z,w$ respectively. Subscripted values such as $z_1, w_1$ denote the (possibly changed) values after a move.

Theorem. Chopsticks is decidably unwinnable: against correct defense, no player can ever force the winning move.

Proof (by contradiction). Assume you win on move $T_n$. Then, without loss of generality, $T_n = 1 \to 3$, so that $$h_n = xyz0 \quad \text{with} \quad x + z \ge 5 ,$$ i.e. the opponent's right hand is already dead and your left hand kills their left. We will show that at $T_{n-1}$ the opponent can always avoid arriving at $h_n = xyz0$ with $x + z \ge 5$.

Case I: $h_{n-1} = xyzw$, $w \neq 0$.

Any $T_{n-1}$ will not lead to the winning case.

Case II: $h_{n-1} = xyz0$, $z > 1$.

Take $T_{n-1} = 3 \to 3,4$. Then $h_n = x\,y\,z_1 w_1$ for $z_1, w_1 \neq 0$.

Case III: $h_{n-1} = 4y10$, $y \neq 4$.

Take $T_{n-1} = 3 \to 1$. Then $h_n = 0y10$ with $y \neq 4$.

Case IV: $h_{n-1} = 4410$.

$$\Rightarrow\; h_{n-2} = 441w, \qquad T_{n-2} = 1 \to 4 \ \text{or}\ 2 \to 4$$ $$\Rightarrow\; h_{n-3} = x_1 41w \ \text{ or }\ 4y_1 1w \ \text{ or }\ 44z_1 w_1 .$$

  1. If $h_{n-3} = x_1 41w$, take $T_{n-3} = 3 \to 2$; then $h_{n-2} = x_1 01w \Rightarrow h_n$ is not the winning case.
  2. If $h_{n-3} = 4y_1 1w$, take $T_{n-3} = 3 \to 1$. Similarly $h_{n-2} = 0y_1 1w \Rightarrow h_n$ is not the winning case.
  3. If $h_{n-3} = 44z_1 w_1$ with $z_1 + w_1 = 1 + w$, take $T_{n-3} = 3 \to 1$; then $h_{n-2} = 04z_1 w_1 \Rightarrow h_n$ is not the winning case.

Case V: $h_{n-1} = xy10$, $x < 3$ or $y < 3$.

Take $T_{n-1} = 3 \to 1$ or $3 \to 2$ respectively; then both $x, y < 4$ $\Rightarrow h_n$ is not the winning case.

Case VI: $h_{n-1} = 3310$.

In this case we can easily avoid the winning case by doing a “trivial move” of $T_{n-1} = 3 \to 3,4$, giving $h_n = 3301$. We call this a trivial move because it does not progress the game at all, and we essentially end up in the same place we started. However, we can still avoid the winning case without utilizing the trivial move. The position $3310$ can only have arisen as $$\big(h_{n-2},\, T_{n-2}\big) \;=\; \big(331w,\ 1 \to 4 \ \text{or}\ 2 \to 4\big),\ w \ge 2 \qquad \text{or} \qquad \big(4210 \ \text{or}\ 2410,\ 1 \to 1,2 \ \text{or}\ 2 \to 2,1\big).$$

  1. $h_{n-2} = 331w$, $w \ge 2$: $$\Rightarrow\; h_{n-3} = 231w \ \text{ or }\ 131w\ (w = 2) \ \text{ or }\ 33z_1 w_1\ (z_1 + w_1 = 1 + w).$$

    1. $h_{n-3} = 231w$ or $131w$: take $T_{n-3} = 4 \to 2$; then $h_{n-2} = 201w$ or $101w \Rightarrow h_n$ not the winning case.
    2. $h_{n-3} = 33z_1 w_1$ with $z_1 + w_1 = 1 + w$: if $z_1 = 0$ or $w_1 = 0$, this can be avoided by Case II. If $z_1, w_1 \neq 0$, then either $z_1 \ge 2$ or $w_1 \ge 2$; so take $T_{n-3} = 3 \to 1$ or $4 \to 1$ respectively $\Rightarrow h_{n-2} = 03z_1 w_1 \Rightarrow h_n$ not the winning case.

  2. $h_{n-2} = 4210$ (or $2410$, symmetrically): $$\Rightarrow\; h_{n-3} = 4201 \ \text{ or }\ 42z_1 w_1\ (z_1 + w_1 = 6) \ \text{ or }\ 3210 \ \text{ or }\ 4110 .$$

    1. $h_{n-3} = 4201$: take $T_{n-3} = 4 \to 1 \Rightarrow h_{n-2} = 0201 \Rightarrow h_n$ not the winning case.
    2. $h_{n-3} = 42z_1 w_1$, $z_1 + w_1 = 6$: take $T_{n-3} = 3 \to 1 \Rightarrow h_{n-2} = 02z_1 w_1 \Rightarrow h_n$ not the winning case.
    3. $h_{n-3} = 3210$: take $T_{n-3} = 3 \to 2 \Rightarrow h_{n-2} = 3310$. Because we are not allowing trivial moves, we cannot have $T_{n-2} : 1 \to 1,2$ giving $h_{n-1} = 3310$. So $h_n$ is not the winning case.
    4. $h_{n-3} = 4110$: take $T_{n-3} = 3 \to 1 \Rightarrow h_{n-2} = 0110 \Rightarrow h_n$ not the winning case.

This covers all cases, and thus shows that an opponent can always escape a winning case. $\blacksquare$
